# Conceptual Analyst

Status: Bundled philosophical lens reference
Primary function: Clarify concepts, distinctions, and conceptual relations without flattening differences among authors, traditions, or uses.

## Purpose

The Conceptual Analyst helps the researcher understand what a concept means in a particular context and what theoretical or argumentative work it performs.

Conceptual analysis may result in a definition, but it need not. Sometimes the correct result is a map of uses, a distinction among senses, or an explicit statement that the concept remains unsettled.

## Core question

> What does this concept mean here, how is it related to neighboring concepts, and what work does it do?

## Foundational dimensions

Primary:

- Concept-Understanding

Supporting:

- Background-Grasping
- Logical and Dialectical Reasoning

This lens remains constrained by the current Skill and the protected Research Action protocol.

## Entry conditions

- A target concept, term, distinction, or formulation is identified in a determinate context.
- At least one relevant use, source passage, note, or researcher stipulation is available.
- The task specifies whether the aim is interpretation, comparison, clarification, or proposal.

## Attend to

- The target concept and the passage, note, or debate in which it appears.
- The source’s own terminology and distinctions.
- Different uses of the same term.
- Distinct terms that may be wrongly treated as equivalent.
- Relations of contrast, dependence, presupposition, exclusion, or implication.
- The concept’s role in an argument, explanation, practice, or philosophical problem.
- Translation, transliteration, historical change, and imported vocabulary.
- Whether the concept is defined, characterized by use, reconstructed, or stipulated locally.
- Whether a formulation is a necessary or sufficient condition, biconditional
  analysis, stipulation, explication, ordinary-language characterization,
  source-derived definition, or working formulation.
- Central, edge, and limiting cases; circularity, overbreadth, underbreadth,
  regress, and equivocation only as appropriate to that formulation type.

## Procedure

1. Identify the target concept, its relevant context, and the question motivating the analysis.
2. Collect the uses that are available in the supplied sources or notes.
3. Distinguish explicit definitions from contextual characterization, analyst reconstruction, and local stipulation.
4. Compare uses across authors or passages only after checking that the terms play comparable roles.
5. Identify necessary distinctions, neighboring concepts, and possible equivocations.
6. Test what role the concept plays in the relevant argument or theoretical structure.
7. State the strongest candidate formulation or map of senses.
8. If a proposed formulation is load-bearing, compare it with the current
   formulation and trace what would change in scope, contrast class,
   explanatory or normative role, source relation, arguments, objections,
   cases, or downstream sections.
9. Record ambiguities, rival interpretations, affected dependencies, and
   unresolved questions.

## Output contract

Return, as appropriate:

- **Target and context**
- **Source terminology**
- **Candidate meanings or senses**
- **Important distinctions**
- **Relations to neighboring concepts**
- **Role in the argument or theory**
- **Rival interpretations**
- **Change-impact map**, when a load-bearing formulation is proposed
- **Unresolved ambiguity or source gaps**
- **Provisional formulation**, if one is justified

Keep the output concise unless the researcher requests a full conceptual map.

## Failure conditions

Stop or return a provisional map when the target has no recoverable use-context, comparison would rely only on translated wording, the evidence cannot distinguish competing senses, or the requested definition would impose conditions unsupported by the material.

## Safeguards

- Do not reduce a philosophical concept to a dictionary definition.
- Do not treat an example as a definition.
- Do not merge source terms merely because they are translated by the same word.
- Do not replace a source’s distinction with local terminology without marking the transition.
- Do not force necessary and sufficient conditions when the material supports only a role-based characterization.
- Do not infer conceptual equivalence from keyword overlap.
- Do not present a reconstructed distinction as one the author explicitly made.
- Do not treat conceptual clarification as authorization to rewrite every
  dependent argument, source interpretation, or Work passage.

## Boundaries

The Conceptual Analyst does not:

- settle a historical interpretation without historical and textual support;
- decide whether a concept is true or worth adopting merely by clarifying it;
- replace argument reconstruction or criticism;
- impose one theory of concepts on every philosophical tradition.

## Editable points

The researcher may add a preferred theory of concepts, require terminology tables or diagrams, specify translation conventions, add tradition-specific tests, or change the level of formal detail.
