## 1. Status and basis

Name the exact target and classify its present status:

- source-explicit position;
- source-supported interpretation;
- reconstructed or charitably repaired position;
- researcher-authored commitment;
- researcher hypothesis or exploration;
- agent proposal;
- disputed or unresolved material.

Identify the evidence and research context that bear on the target. A note records the researcher's current formulation; it is not independent evidence for what a source says. An earlier agent response is not a settled commitment merely because it remains in Dialogue.

Do not develop around an unverified attribution. If source interpretation is load-bearing and uncertain, stop for source analysis. Use a researcher-installed citation skill when the unresolved issue specifically concerns a citation system, edition, locator convention, or bibliographic record.

## 2. Concept development

Classify the formulation before evaluating it:

- necessary condition;
- sufficient condition;
- biconditional analysis;
- stipulation;
- explication or rational reconstruction;
- ordinary-language characterization;
- source-derived definition;
- operational or working formulation;
- inferential-role or practice-based characterization.

Then examine:

- scope and contrast class;
- role in the argument or practice;
- neighboring, opposed, and easily conflated concepts;
- necessary, sufficient, defeasible, or enabling conditions when appropriate;
- modality, normative force, and level of abstraction;
- ambiguity, equivocation, metaphor, and shifts in use;
- central, edge, impaired, and limiting cases where relevant;
- circularity, regress, overbreadth, and underbreadth;
- explanatory gains and theoretical costs;
- consequences for sources, arguments, objections, and other commitments.

Do not force every philosophical concept into a dictionary-style definition. Some are best understood through inferential role, genealogy, contrast, phenomenological articulation, historical use, or practical function.

Never treat a slogan, example, consequence, diagnostic mark, or necessary condition as a complete definition without justification.

## 3. Argument development

Reconstruct the form that best represents the reasoning rather than imposing deductive notation. Record:

```text
Target conclusion:
Premises, grounds, passages, cases, or explanatory considerations:
Support type:
Inferential or interpretive link:
Scope, modality, and claimed force:
Bridge premises:
Hidden assumptions:
Burden of proof:
Premise support:
Dependencies:
```

Diagnose whether weakness lies in:

- a false, unsupported, or dialectically unavailable premise;
- invalid or weak inference;
- missing bridge;
- ambiguous term or concept drift;
- overstrong conclusion;
- scope, modal, temporal, quantifier, or explanatory-level shift;
- burden shift or circularity;
- mismatch between evidence and claim;
- conflict with another commitment.

Distinguish validity, premise truth or support, explanatory power, dialectical availability, and overall defensibility. Do not reject an argument merely because an opponent disputes a premise; identify the burden created by the dispute.

For formal reasoning, check definitions, assumptions, derivation, models or countermodels, and the mapping to the natural-language claim. For normative reasoning, identify the normative target, force, bridge, conflicts, priority, defeat, feasibility, and residual demand. For cases, identify stipulations, imported assumptions, modal role, target, verdict, variants, and inferential function.

## 4. Dialectical development

Generate only objections with a determinate target:

- thesis or conclusion;
- premise or bridge premise;
- inference;
- concept or definition;
- interpretation or source use;
- case description or judgment;
- scope condition;
- method or explanatory burden;
- conflict with another commitment.

Mark each objection as source-raised, opponent-attributed, researcher-generated, or agent-generated, and as internal or external. Present the strongest relevant version before replying.

A reply may deny a premise, distinguish cases, challenge the inference, reinterpret the target, narrow the thesis, accept a cost, expose a presupposition, or concede a weaker conclusion. State what the reply preserves, gives up, and leaves under pressure. Restating the thesis is not a reply.

## 5. Dependencies and alternatives

Trace only evidenced dependencies. Do not infer substantive dependence from proximity, linking, or shared vocabulary.

Compare alternatives by:

- explanatory or interpretive gain;
- conceptual cost;
- source fidelity;
- inferential strength;
- scope and applicability;
- objection resistance;
- compatibility with other commitments;
- revision cost and unresolved pressure.

Do not choose among substantive alternatives on the researcher's behalf. Recommend only when the reasons and tradeoffs are explicit, and label the recommendation as an agent evaluation.

## 6. Failure conditions

Stop or return candidates when:

- the target depends on an unavailable or unverified source;
- the current formulation cannot be distinguished from the source's view;
- a proposed repair introduces a new premise or definition without labeling it;
- authorization covers one target but not a dependent target whose omission would create a misleading record;
- the task has become prose drafting or independent review;
- the available material does not determine whether the conflict is conceptual, textual, inferential, or methodological.
