## 1. Evidence hierarchy

Use the strongest available evidence appropriate to the claim:

1. primary paper, original philosophical text, or authoritative edition inspected with sufficient context;
2. verified quotation or excerpt with a stable locator and adequate surrounding context;
3. source-analysis report whose claims are locator-backed or explicitly marked as reconstruction;
4. current researcher-authored notes for the researcher's own commitments;
5. general philosophical background knowledge, used cautiously and never as evidence for a source-specific attribution.

Project notes, prior agent responses, memory, and synthesis maps are context, not evidence of what a published source says. Unread bibliography items are leads.

When the best required source is missing, mark the finding `source missing` or `unverified`. Do not fill the gap from familiarity.

## 2. Claim classification

Classify each substantive item as one or more of:

- concept definition or distinction;
- source-explicit claim;
- source's report of another view;
- interpretive or historical claim;
- phenomenological description;
- formal premise, derivation, or formal-to-prose bridge;
- normative claim or bridge;
- case or thought-experiment role;
- source-supported reconstruction;
- agent-supplied charitable repair;
- argument-reconstruction claim;
- evidential-role or source-role claim;
- comparative claim;
- critical claim;
- synthetic research claim;
- researcher commitment;
- agent proposal.

The classification determines what evidence and philosophical checks are appropriate.

## 3. Audit tests

For each claim, assign:

- `verified`;
- `partly_supported`;
- `overstated`;
- `unsupported`;
- `source_missing`.

Then test:

- Does the source support the exact wording, strength, and scope?
- Does each claim remain within the target's declared Research Unit, and does that declaration accurately describe the material actually represented?
- Is a report, reconstruction, repair, criticism, or synthesis presented as textual fact?
- Are terms preserved before being translated into local vocabulary?
- Are distinct source terms collapsed into one concept?
- Are scope, modality, normative force, explanatory force, and burden stable?
- Does the stated conclusion follow from the reconstructed support?
- Are objections, examples, concessions, and replies assigned their real roles?
- Does a citation support the premise for which it is used?
- Are reliable locators present for source-specific claims in durable content?
- Is uncertainty visible where the evidence is incomplete or contested?

Separate internal criticism from external evaluation. Separate a source's debate narrative from independently verified field history.

## 4. Genre-sensitive checks

### Historical and interpretive work

Check passage context, edition, translation, chronology, conceptual change, reception, interpretive principle, rival reading, and anachronism.

### Phenomenological work

Preserve the distinction between description and explanatory, causal, metaphysical, normative, or empirical inference.

### Formal work

Check definitions, assumptions, derivation, models, interpretation, scope, and the bridge back to prose.

### Normative work

Check normative target, bearer, force, reasons or values, bridge principles, priority, conflicts, defeat, feasibility, and residual demands.

### Cases and thought experiments

Check stipulations, imported assumptions, target, modal role, intended verdict, rival descriptions, variants, and whether the case illustrates, supports, diagnoses, pressures, or refutes.

### Empirically informed work

Keep scientific claim, technical reliability, warranted scope, bridge premise, and philosophical conclusion separate. Mark technical questions for specialist verification.

## 5. Common defects

Flag explicitly:

- false attribution;
- unsupported source or dialectical role;
- source selected only by keyword overlap;
- concept substitution or terminological assimilation;
- collapse of distinct source terms;
- scope, modal, normative, or explanatory drift;
- opponent's view presented as the author's;
- reconstruction or repair presented as quotation or textual fact;
- agent proposal presented as researcher commitment;
- example, slogan, or consequence presented as a definition;
- background source presented as direct support;
- unread lead promoted into evidence;
- unsupported relation such as support, influence, criticism, supersession, or equivalence;
- synthesis or note presented as primary-source evidence;
- empirical result treated as sufficient for a conceptual, normative, metaphysical, or interpretive conclusion;
- missing locator for a source-specific claim;
- a claim that exceeds the declared Research Unit, or a Research Unit that overstates the source material actually analyzed.

Do not invent defects to make the audit appear rigorous.

## 6. Verdicts and revision

Use severity:

- `BLOCKER` — central false attribution, serious conceptual misuse, invalid reconstruction, or unsupported evidential role;
- `MAJOR` — significant overstatement, ambiguity, or missing support;
- `MINOR` — basically accurate but needing qualification, narrower wording, or locator repair;
- `SUGGESTION` — optional improvement, not an accuracy defect.

Revise only when authorized. Preserve the researcher's thesis when a narrower accurate fix is possible. If accuracy requires a substantive change, present the change and its philosophical consequences separately for researcher decision.

An audit `Pass` means no material defect was found within the stated scope and available evidence. It does not guarantee truth, originality, philosophical importance, or completeness.
