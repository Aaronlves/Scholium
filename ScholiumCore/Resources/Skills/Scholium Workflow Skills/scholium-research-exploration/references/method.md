# Research Exploration Method

Use this method to make a philosophical problem intelligible before trying to solve or write it.

## 1. Fix the initial question

Record the researcher's wording before improving it. Identify:

- the object of inquiry;
- the relevant debate or domain;
- the intended level of analysis: textual, historical, conceptual, normative, formal, metaphysical, epistemological, phenomenological, methodological, or mixed;
- relevant time, tradition, language, or source boundaries;
- the intended use of the exploration.

Offer reformulations as alternatives. For each reformulation, state what it preserves, excludes, or changes. Do not silently replace an unfamiliar question with a familiar one.

## 2. Reconstruct the background problem

Map only what the available material supports:

- the pressure or puzzle that motivates the debate;
- why philosophers regard it as requiring an answer;
- assumptions that make competing answers attractive or necessary;
- historical or theoretical developments that changed the problem;
- whether apparent participants answer the same question.

Knowing who said what is not yet background-grasping. Explain why the positions arise and why their differences matter.

Distinguish the debate's self-presentation in a source from independently established field history. Treat unread references and remembered literature as leads.

## 3. Map positions through commitments and costs

For each important position or strategy, identify:

- the question it answers;
- its central commitment;
- its motivation or attraction;
- what it explains, preserves, or makes possible;
- what it must deny, revise, or leave unexplained;
- its strongest apparent pressure;
- whether the position has several importantly different versions.

Do not flatten a family of views into one slogan. Do not infer agreement from shared vocabulary or disagreement from opposed wording.

## 4. Diagnose the apparent tension

Test whether the tension arises from:

- different senses of a term;
- different contrast classes;
- different questions or explanatory levels;
- different standards of evidence or success;
- different historical settings;
- incompatible commitments;
- an unresolved bridge between two kinds of claim.

State what would count as resolving, dissolving, reframing, or deepening the tension.

## 5. Assess philosophical stakes

Treat significance as a reasoned assessment, not a compliment. Ask:

- What changes if one answer is accepted rather than another?
- Which broader commitments or practices are affected?
- Does the issue expose a hidden assumption, explanatory limit, conceptual instability, normative cost, or conflict among attractive commitments?
- Would resolving it clarify a larger debate or merely rename an existing choice?
- What is the cost of ignoring or misframing the problem?

Separate intrinsic philosophical interest, project relevance, historical importance, and likely publication interest. Do not infer one from another.

## 6. Generate candidate directions

Generate only directions grounded in the reconstructed problem. For each candidate, state:

```text
Candidate question or claim:
Problem addressed:
Background or source basis:
Why it may matter:
Strongest obvious risk:
Evidence or analysis needed:
What would count against it:
```

Prefer a few determinate candidates over an inventory of fashionable topics. Mark all novelty and importance claims as unverified unless the necessary literature work has been done.

## 7. Select a bounded next step

Choose one small action that can materially reduce uncertainty:

- inspect a named primary source;
- verify a historical or attribution claim;
- distinguish two concepts;
- reconstruct one argument;
- compare two positions at the same level;
- test one objection or counterexample;
- produce a bounded synthesis map.

Do not convert exploratory uncertainty into a broad automatic research plan.

## Failure conditions

Stop or narrow the output when:

- the background map depends on sources not inspected;
- the candidate topic is supported only by keyword overlap;
- the comparison joins questions at different levels without a bridge;
- a novelty or importance verdict lacks a defensible basis;
- the task has become source interpretation, writing, or review and should move to the corresponding Workflow Skill.
