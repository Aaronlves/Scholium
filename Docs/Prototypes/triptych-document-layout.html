Double Effect and Moral Description

The principle of double effect is most useful when treated as a claim about the structure of agency, not as a device that mechanically classifies outcomes.

1. Why description comes first

A moral assessment does not begin with two independently specified outcomes and then ask which one was intended. It begins with an account of what the agent was doing, which reasons organized the action, and which descriptions belong to the plan itself.

This makes the dispute partly conceptual. Two readers may agree about every physical consequence while disagreeing about the action under consideration. The disagreement concerns the practical description under which the agent acts.

Statement

Intention is not an extra mental item attached to behavior; it helps determine the action that is available for moral evaluation.

2. The pressure from redescription

If an agent may redescribe a chosen means as a merely foreseen side effect, the distinction loses normative force. A defensible version therefore needs constraints on admissible descriptions. Those constraints cannot be supplied by vocabulary alone.

The relevant test asks whether the alleged side effect figures in the agent’s practical route to the end. A bounded conclusion

The analysis supports a narrower conclusion than a general defense of double effect: descriptions of action must be disciplined by the agent’s practical reasoning. Whether that discipline vindicates the doctrine remains an open question.
