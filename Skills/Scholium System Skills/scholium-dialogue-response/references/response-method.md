# Dialogue Response Method

## 1. Build the base outcome

Begin with one compact statement that answers what the work achieved academically.

- For a successful edit, describe the change in interpretation, argument, conceptual structure, evidential role, organization, or prose function.
- For read-only work, describe the conclusion, reconstruction, comparison, or finding.
- For partial work, distinguish completed work from what remains.
- For stopped work, state the research consequence of the blocker before technical detail.

Do not use routine operations as the subject of the outcome. “Clarified the distinction between X and Y” is appropriate; “updated three files” is not sufficient.

## 2. Add selected modules

Use the stored order unless two modules would repeat the same content. In that case, retain distinct headings but avoid restating the same point.

### Critical Reflection

Identify the strongest warranted vulnerability, assumption, ambiguity, tension, counterexample, interpretive risk, or cost. Explain why it matters. Distinguish a demonstrated defect from a possible concern and do not manufacture criticism merely to satisfy the module.

### Remaining Questions

Name only questions that remain open after the completed work and bear directly on the researcher’s task. Do not turn generic possibilities into apparent research obligations.

### Philosophical Significance

Explain what conceptual, argumentative, methodological, or practical stake makes the result matter. State conditional significance conditionally. Do not inflate novelty or importance beyond the available background.

### Debate Context

Locate the result within the relevant problem, positions, motivations, commitments, and costs only to the extent supported by material actually checked. Distinguish source-grounded context from the agent’s broader reconstruction.

### Research Directions

Suggest a small number of bounded next investigations tied to a specific unresolved question, evidential gap, objection, or conceptual pressure. Do not expand the active task or present a suggestion as already authorized work.

## 3. Preserve mandatory integrity notices

Add a compact limitation or researcher-decision statement whenever needed, regardless of selected modules. Always disclose:

- unavailable, partial, or unreliable source access;
- an unsupported or disputed attribution;
- a stale fingerprint, conflict, or failed persistence step;
- an unresolved choice that would alter the researcher’s thesis or settled note;
- an audit still needed for the exact changed version.

Do not create a separate heading when one sentence in the relevant module is clearer.

## 4. Keep the Response concise

- Use one or two sentences for the base outcome.
- Use at most one short paragraph or three items for each selected module.
- Prefer no more than one decisive issue per optional module unless the task genuinely requires more.
- Omit empty headings.
- Avoid repeating note titles and source lists already visible in Dialogue unless identification prevents ambiguity.

## 5. Example

```markdown
**Academic Outcome**

The Topic note now distinguishes the source’s constitutive claim about reasons from the agent’s causal-explanatory reconstruction and treats the proposed bridge between them as a hypothesis rather than a source-established equivalence.

**Critical Reflection**

The revised account still depends on a bridge premise that the checked material does not defend; the stronger explanatory conclusion therefore remains provisional.

**Remaining Questions**

- Does the later chapter defend the bridge premise or merely presuppose it?
- Would a noncausal account explain the same normative dependence with fewer commitments?
```

The example illustrates structure only. Never reuse its claims without relevant evidence.
