## Purpose and ownership

Use this method when producing a meaning-preserving revision of academic philosophical prose: articles, chapters, proposals, literature reviews, objections and replies, revised academic paragraphs, and polished note-insertion candidates.

This method governs prose movement only. The active Workflow Skill, source method, philosophical Practice, citation convention, and application protocol govern non-prose requirements.

Treat this file as an editable researcher-owned profile. Preserve the adopted researcher's explicit style choices rather than presenting them as universal standards of good philosophy.

## Style models

The starter profile draws three abstract prose habits from named models:

1. **Naar** — dialectical pacing, careful qualification, and pressure-driven transitions.
2. **Tappolet** — clear question formulation, useful taxonomies, and accessible distinctions.
3. **Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy topic entries** — topic orientation, route maps, and scope boundaries at larger scales.

Do not imitate any model's content, terminology, conclusions, or impersonal voice. Extract prose habits only: how a paragraph begins, how a question is framed, how a contrast is introduced, how objections are staged, and how conclusions remain modest.

Use a named model text directly only when it belongs to the authorized read set. Otherwise rely on the abstract profile above and state no claim of direct imitation. A researcher may replace these models in an adopted copy.

## Core movement

Make the existing thesis or controlling claim visible through a live philosophical question. A section should usually:

1. introduce the philosophical pressure already motivating the passage;
2. state the question raised by that pressure;
3. distinguish nearby views or tempting formulations already relevant to the inquiry;
4. explain why those options are too strong, too weak, or aimed at the wrong target when the existing content supports that diagnosis;
5. motivate the passage's established preferred formulation;
6. mark what remains conditional, provisional, disputed, or left for later.

Prefer plain philosophical sentences over ornate phrasing. Keep the prose patient, direct, and rigorous, but do not over-polish it in a way that hides the argument or smooths away unresolved pressure.

In Prose Control, these are organizational possibilities, not permission to invent missing philosophical content. Omit a move when the original passage does not contain it.

## Question-focused exposition

Use this pattern when a passage explains a debate, concept, or field rather than immediately defending one premise.

A question-focused section should usually:

1. begin with a compact characterization of the topic;
2. state why the topic generates a philosophical question;
3. divide the terrain into a small number of genuine live questions;
4. introduce positions as answers to those questions;
5. compare positions by the pressure each solves and the pressure it leaves;
6. use existing cases, distinctions, and examples to clarify the pressure;
7. state the scope boundary when neighboring debates are relevant but not part of the present topic;
8. end by explaining what remains open or why the next question arises.

Do not organize expository prose as a chronology, author catalogue, or bibliography in prose form unless chronology itself carries explanatory weight. Authors enter because they formulate a question, defend a position, expose a difficulty, supply a distinction, or mark a transition.

## Route-mapping openings

A strong opening quickly gives the reader:

1. a working characterization of the topic;
2. the live question or family of questions;
3. the route by which the section will answer or sharpen those questions;
4. any scope boundary needed to prevent misunderstanding.

Useful shape:

```text
The topic is X, initially understood as the problem of Y. It raises two questions. The first concerns whether X can explain Z. The second concerns whether that explanation can survive W. I begin with the first issue, because it fixes the target of the later objection.
```

Use this shape for a substantial introduction, topic-note opening, or literature-review overview when the underlying content supports it. Do not impose it on a short argumentative paragraph.

## Question-to-pressure movement

In question-focused exposition, prefer this sequence when the source passage already contains the required elements:

1. state the local question;
2. give the natural first answer;
3. explain why that answer is attractive;
4. introduce a rival or corrective answer;
5. identify the remaining pressure;
6. say whether the pressure belongs to the present topic or a neighboring topic;
7. hand the reader to the next section.

This movement prevents exposition from becoming a flat list. The reader should see why each paragraph is needed by the question that precedes it.

## Contrastive formulation

When introducing a view, state the contrast that makes it intelligible rather than merely naming it.

Useful shapes:

```text
The issue is not merely whether X is connected to Y. The question is whether that connection can support a more substantive account of Z.
```

```text
One way to resist this conclusion is to deny X. A different way is to grant X but deny that it has the significance the objection assumes.
```

Use contrastive formulation when a view is easily confused with a neighboring view or when the reader needs to know what exactly is being denied. Do not manufacture a contrast absent from the original commitment.

## Option and burden mapping

When a debate has genuinely different routes, separate them explicitly.

Useful shape:

```text
There are three ways to resist this picture. The first denies the starting assumption. The second accepts the assumption but rejects the inference. The third grants both but denies that the result is problematic.
```

Use this structure only when the alternatives are genuinely different. Do not create artificial lists for material better handled as one contrast. After mapping options, state the burden each option accepts when the original analysis establishes those burdens.

## Too strong, too weak, or wrong target

When the passage rejects a nearby position, name the kind of failure involved:

- **Too strong** — the view gets the desired result only by accepting an implausible or revisionary claim.
- **Too weak** — the view identifies a real connection but does not explain the target phenomenon.
- **Wrong target** — the view explains a related phenomenon rather than the one currently at issue.

Use this diagnosis for objections, definition work, and transitions into a positive account. Do not assign a diagnosis merely because it produces a neat paragraph.

## Objection-led development

Use objections to clarify the burden of the existing view.

A good objection-reply movement should:

1. state the objection in its strongest already-supported form;
2. identify the assumption on which it depends;
3. grant what the passage can grant;
4. deny or qualify the crucial step;
5. explain what remains of the original thesis.

Avoid replies that merely restate the thesis. A reply must change the dialectical situation. If the original reply does not, Prose Control should expose that defect and keep any repair as a separate candidate.

## Constructive proposals after failed alternatives

Place a positive view after the reader understands why the established alternatives are inadequate.

Useful shape:

```text
This suggests a different way to formulate the view. Instead of saying that X is identical with Y, I can say that X plays the role that made the analogy attractive in the first place.
```

The proposal should answer the exact pressure raised earlier. In Prose Control, do not create the proposal; use this structure only when the researcher's existing content already supplies it.

## Paragraph discipline

Give each paragraph one main function:

- context;
- pressure;
- distinction;
- alternative;
- objection;
- reply;
- positive proposal;
- transition;
- limitation.

Long paragraphs are acceptable when the dialectical movement requires them, but they must not perform unrelated jobs. Split a paragraph that both introduces a debate and replies to an objection unless their unity is philosophically necessary.

A strong paragraph normally contains:

1. a local claim;
2. the reason for that claim;
3. the contrast or distinction needed to prevent misunderstanding;
4. the consequence for the larger argument.

Do not add an absent reason or consequence merely to complete the pattern.

## Transition discipline

Use transitions to explain why the next move is needed. Avoid transitions that merely announce sequence.

Weak:

```text
I now turn to another issue.
```

Better when warranted by the passage:

```text
This first reply avoids the strongest version of the objection, but it leaves unexplained why the relevant response has normative significance. I therefore turn to that further issue.
```

Use explicit dialectical markers where useful: `one might`, `of course`, `that said`, `however`, `if this is right`, `the question is whether`, `this suggests`, `the difficulty is`, `the worry is not`, and `for present purposes`.

Do not insert these markers mechanically. Each must name a real argumentative, interpretive, explanatory, or dialectical relation.

## Literature-review prose

Organize literature by problem, position, pressure, and remaining gap rather than by author catalogue.

Use authors to locate argumentative roles:

- who introduces the problem;
- who gives the strongest available formulation;
- who exposes a pressure;
- who supplies a distinction or case;
- who leaves a gap that the current inquiry may address.

Do not present the researcher's own contribution as part of the established literature. A literature review may motivate the contribution, but it must not smuggle that contribution into the survey or turn an unverified novelty claim into field history.

## Claim modesty

Use controlled first-person claims when they mark real researcher commitments:

- `I argue` for a defended conclusion;
- `I propose` for a formulation being introduced;
- `I suggest` for a weaker or exploratory move;
- `I leave open` for a deliberately unresolved issue.

Do not infer adoption merely from the convenience of first-person prose. Preserve the original authorial and epistemic status.

Avoid saying that an argument or view “shows” more than it establishes. Prefer `suggests`, `supports`, `motivates`, `puts pressure on`, or `helps explain` when the support is partial.

Mark limits explicitly:

- what is conditional on an assumption;
- what has not yet been argued;
- what is left for later work;
- what the present passage does not need to settle.

## Modest closure

End a section by stating what has and has not been established.

Useful shape:

```text
This does not yet show that the view is correct. It shows only that the familiar objection does not force us back to the original alternative.
```

Use this closure when the section sketches a position, opens a route, or motivates later work rather than completing the argument. Do not understate a conclusion the researcher has actually established merely to sound cautious.

## Revision checks

Before treating the prose as controlled, check:

- Does the opening make the existing live philosophical pressure visible?
- Is the main question explicit when the passage contains one?
- Does each paragraph have one defensible main function?
- Are nearby views distinguished before criticism?
- Are rejected options diagnosed accurately rather than tidily?
- Does the positive proposal answer the pressure that motivated it?
- Are transitions explanatory rather than merely sequential?
- Are claims no stronger or weaker than the original support permits?
- Does the revision preserve concepts, definitions, inferences, objections, replies, source roles, scope, modality, qualification, and status?
- Does it preserve unresolved issues instead of smoothing them away?
- Are substantive repairs clearly outside the controlled revision?
- Does the prose still sound like the researcher rather than the agent or a model text?
