# Foundational Dimensions

Status: Shared reference  
Scope: Common philosophical capacities for Scholium Philosophical Practices

## Relationship to the Core Protocol

The protected `scholium-core-protocol` is the sole authority for fidelity, precision, accuracy, non-fabrication, transparency, researcher authority, task scope, and permission. A researcher may edit this Practice library, but no edit here can weaken or replace that universal boundary.

The **foundational dimensions** below describe philosophical capacities that Practices exercise to different degrees. Primary and supporting mappings indicate emphasis only. They do not rank the capacities, certify philosophical quality, or imply that an optional Practice can serve as evidence.

## Foundational dimensions

### 1. Background-Grasping

Understand the intellectual landscape in which a philosophical question arises.

This includes:

- knowing the main positions and relevant interlocutors;
- understanding why philosophers care about the question;
- identifying the motivations, commitments, benefits, and costs of positions;
- recognizing historical and theoretical pressures;
- distinguishing genuine disagreement from parties addressing different questions.

Background-Grasping is not an inventory of who said what. It explains why the debate has its shape and why its alternatives matter.

### 2. Concept-Understanding

Understand philosophical concepts, distinctions, relations, and theoretical roles.

This includes:

- identifying candidate meanings and uses;
- preserving distinctions among neighboring concepts;
- recognizing ambiguity, equivocation, and conceptual drift;
- understanding what work a concept performs in an argument or theory;
- comparing concepts across authors or traditions without assuming equivalence.

Concept-Understanding does not require every concept to have necessary and sufficient conditions. The appropriate result may be a role-based account, a map of uses, or an explicitly unresolved interpretation.

### 3. Philosophical Taste

Recognize questions, tensions, and possible accounts that are philosophically significant or promising.

This includes:

- identifying what is genuinely at stake;
- distinguishing deep tensions from merely verbal or technical complications;
- recognizing surprising consequences or neglected assumptions;
- selecting fruitful problems and lines of development;
- proposing accounts whose interest can be explained rather than merely asserted.

Philosophical Taste remains a matter of judgment. An agent should provide reasons why a topic or proposal may be interesting, not declare importance or novelty as an objective fact.

### 4. Logical and Dialectical Reasoning

Understand and evaluate reasons, inferences, objections, replies, burdens, and consequences.

This includes:

- identifying premises, conclusions, assumptions, and inferential links;
- distinguishing validity, soundness, plausibility, explanatory adequacy, and evidential support;
- reconstructing the strongest relevant version of a position;
- formulating serious objections rather than strawmen;
- comparing replies, concessions, revisions, and their costs;
- tracking how a claim changes the surrounding dialectical position.

Logical and Dialectical Reasoning is broader than formal validity. Formalization should be used when it clarifies the reasoning and avoided when it distorts the philosophical task.

## Mapping rule

Every practice file should declare:

- **Primary dimensions** — capacities central to the practice’s purpose.
- **Supporting dimensions** — capacities the practice relies on or develops secondarily.

Every Practice remains governed by the protected Core Protocol whether or not a safeguard is repeated in that file.

## Anti-expansion rule

Add a new foundational dimension only when it identifies a distinct philosophical capacity that cannot be adequately represented by the existing four. Add a new practice only when it represents a recurring philosophical activity that cannot be adequately performed by composing existing practices.
