## 1. Calibrate the review

Identify the work's philosophical task: defend, interpret, distinguish, explain, diagnose, recover, dissolve, synthesize, apply, or reframe. Calibrate standards to its genre:

- analytic or argument-driven;
- historical, exegetical, or intellectual-historical;
- phenomenological or descriptive;
- genealogical, critical, or diagnostic;
- formal;
- normative;
- conceptual engineering or explication;
- case- or thought-experiment-driven;
- empirically informed;
- hybrid.

Do not force every work into deductive argument, scientific-paper structure, or novelty-as-gap-filling. State any standard not supplied by the work or researcher.

Fix the exact version, target, audience, stage, and usable locators. If the target is changing or incomplete, narrow the review or qualify the verdict.

## 2. Build the philosophical map

Before criticism, reconstruct:

- question, aim, and central thesis or controlling interpretation;
- concepts, definitions, distinctions, scope, and method;
- arguments, textual grounds, descriptions, cases, formal moves, or normative bridges;
- dialectical targets, objections, replies, concessions, and limitations;
- source roles and literature-placement claims;
- claimed contribution and significance.

If a charitable reconstruction requires a new premise or argument, label it as a possible repair. Do not criticize an invented version when the work itself remains underdetermined.

## 3. Apply independent lenses

Record each lens's findings before synthesis.

### Lens 1: Conceptual and methodological

Test:

- clarity and stability of thesis, concepts, definitions, distinctions, scope, modality, and contrast class;
- fit between method and aim;
- ambiguity, equivocation, concept drift, circularity, overbreadth, and underbreadth;
- support appropriate to each load-bearing premise;
- central, edge, impaired, or limiting cases where relevant.

Do not demand independent defense of a stipulation or opponent-granted premise unless its use is itself contested.

### Lens 2: Argumentative and inferential

Test:

- the claimed kind of support;
- premises, bridge premises, inference, force, burden, and hidden assumptions;
- modal, quantifier, temporal, normative, or explanatory-level shifts;
- whether the conclusion exceeds its grounds;
- strongest objection, reply strategy, concession, and residual pressure.

Do not force phenomenological, genealogical, interpretive, abductive, or case-based reasoning into deductive notation.

### Lens 3: Interpretive and source-fidelity

Test:

- passage-level support and context;
- separation of source-explicit claims, reports, reconstruction, repair, and evaluation;
- edition, translation, chronology, reception, or anachronism issues;
- rival readings;
- source terminology and role attribution;
- whether an empirical or secondary source supports the exact claim assigned to it.

### Lens 4: Dialectical and contribution

Test:

- the live pressure organizing the work;
- relevant rivals, partial allies, alternatives, and objections;
- internal versus external criticism;
- burden created, shifted, or discharged;
- contribution type and its actual significance;
- implications, limits, and material literature omissions.

Do not equate contribution with novelty. Interpretation, clarification, synthesis, diagnosis, recovery, and dissolution can be genuine contributions.

### Lens 5: Exposition and audience

Test whether the intended reader can identify the problem, aim, route, claims, and burdens. Assess section function, paragraph movement, terminology, examples, transitions, accessibility, voice, and citation consistency only after substance.

Do not penalize specialist writing for assuming background the intended audience may reasonably share.

## 4. Verify source-dependent findings

Verify when a finding materially depends on attribution, interpretation, literature placement, novelty, current debate, quotation, historical claim, or empirical premise.

Use sources in this order when applicable:

1. primary text or original work;
2. verified passage with sufficient context;
3. relevant peer-reviewed scholarship or authoritative commentary;
4. professionally edited reference work for orientation;
5. project notes only for the researcher's commitments, never as evidence of a published source's view.

For empirical premises, separate scientific result, warranted scope, bridge premise, and philosophical conclusion. Mark technical validity for domain-specific checking when needed.

If verification is unavailable, narrow the finding and label it provisional or unverified.

## 5. Adjudicate findings

For every major finding require:

```text
Finding ID and severity:
Exact target and location:
Work's move:
Problem or pressure:
Internal or external status:
Evidence and reasoning:
Consequence:
Strongest reply or repair:
Cost and residual pressure:
Source check needed:
```

Use severity:

- `BLOCKER` — the central aim fails and no plausible local repair is visible;
- `MAJOR` — a central burden needs substantial defense, reconstruction, or narrowing;
- `MODERATE` — the project is viable but a significant local burden remains;
- `MINOR` — a localized qualification, clarification, locator, transition, or presentation repair;
- `SUGGESTION` — optional improvement rather than a defect.

Convergence across lenses is a prioritization signal, not confirmation. Preserve a decisive minority objection and reject repeated findings based on a shared misreading.

## 6. Recommendations and output

Use ordinary version-bound recommendations:

- no major revision required;
- limited revision;
- major revision;
- fundamental reconsideration;
- unverified.

Use journal labels only in `referee-simulation`. Never convert a review recommendation into persistent readiness, approval, submission, or truth status.

Lead with the strongest contribution and decisive reasons. Then provide prioritized findings, lens disagreements, ordered revision burdens, source limitations, and researcher decisions. Do not pad the report to produce a fixed number of strengths or weaknesses.
